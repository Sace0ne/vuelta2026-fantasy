🚴 Stage 3 — Route Intel
The Vuelta's first summit finish, and an unusually gentle one. Seventy kilometres of pancake-flat coastal road open the stage — ideal breakaway terrain, with echelon risk along the exposed Mediterranean shore. The route skirts the wetlands past Perpignan, turns south-west through vineyards, then picks up the Têt valley through Villefranche-de-Conflent for the intermediate sprint at km 129.

Everything then happens at once. Col de Mont-Louis (cat. 1, 19.4 km at 4.8%, summit km 157.8) is long rather than steep — no ramp on it bites, but sustained tempo at altitude does its own damage. The two climbs function as one effort: roughly 27 km at 4.5%, with no gradient anywhere suiting a pure explosive attack. This rewards sustained power over punch, and a strong team can control it in a way the Vuelta's usual walls don't allow.

2,639 m of climbing, almost all inside the final 35 km. Rain forecast on the finishing climbs.
🏁 THE FINAL 16 KM
Over the top of Mont-Louis with 16.2 km left, the road runs dead straight to the south-west — flat at first, then tipping downhill. A brief, deceptive lull: barely enough to recover, easily long enough for a dropped rider to claw back on.

At the bottom the route swings north-west onto Font-Romeu (cat. 2, 9.8 km at 5%), turning through north-west, north-east and north as it climbs — so whatever the wind is doing, it will do something different on each section.

It breaks into three efforts. The opening 2 km at 3–4% are the softest ramp on the climb and the obvious place to be caught napping. The middle 7 km at a relentless 5–6% is where the stage is decided, on a twisting road through three hairpins that repeatedly hide the group ahead. Then it eases to 4.4% for the final 900 m, after a roundabout with 1,900 m to go.

That closing kick matters for picking a winner: a rider who cracks in the middle section won't recover, but the shallow final kilometre gives anyone still in contact a real chance to come back over the top. A finish that can be lost early and won late.
🚴 Stage 4 — Route Intel
Barely a hundred kilometres, and the hardest day of the opening week. The road climbs from the gun: Port d'Envalira, Andorra's longest ascent, swallows the first 23–25 km at around 5% with ramps to 11%, topping out above 2,400 m — the worst of it saved for the final third, at altitude that hurts everyone.

A 25 km descent, then straight back up. Collada de Beixalís (6.5 km at 8.5%, pitches to 16%) is the steepest thing here. Coll d'Ordino follows almost immediately, 9.9 km at 7%, and with both summits near 2,000 m this is where the serious attacks land.

No flat road anywhere to recover on. Around 3,024 m of climbing in 104 km, and the first stage where the GC riders genuinely can't hide.
🏁 THE FINAL 26 KM
Over Ordino with 26.1 km left, the descent is technical at first then eases, curling north-east, south and south-west back toward the outskirts of Andorra la Vella. The finish is right there — and the route deliberately turns away from it.

Instead the road swings south and south-west onto Alto de la Comella (cat. 3, 3.8 km at 5.5%), a climb whose average is a lie. The first kilometre is 9.7% — brutal, and after Envalira, Beixalís and Ordino it will hurt far more than the number suggests. The middle flattens almost entirely, offering a genuine chance to close a gap, before the last 1.3 km at 8.6% delivers the final blow. Summit at km 100.

Then 4.8 km of intensely technical descending — north-west, south-west, west, north-east — dropping into the capital. A gap made on the Comella can be lost here by anyone who hesitates in the corners, and equally a good descender can bridge. The last 400 m tick slightly upward, with a sharp turn at a roundabout with 400 m to go and another at 300 m.

Whoever crests the Comella first has the best shot — but only if their nerve holds on the way down.
🚴 Stage 5 — Route Intel
Billed as hilly, built for a bunch kick. The route runs toward L'Ampolla and some of the flattest kilometres of the entire Vuelta, with uncategorised lumps through the final fifty before the day's only real obstacle, the cat. 3 Alto de Paüls.

Just 1,689 m of climbing — comfortably the easiest day so far. Roquetes, in the Terres de l'Ebre between the Ebro and the Els Ports massif, hosts a Vuelta finish for the first time.
🏁 THE FINAL 25 KM
Paüls tops out at km 148.4, and with 24.9 km left everything gets easier — which is exactly why the breakaway's odds shrink from here. A gentle descent runs south-west then south-east back down to the river, joining a straight, flat road that follows the water toward Tortosa.

A final turn north-west leads into the finish, the last kilometre rising gently at 1.4% — not enough to trouble a sprinter, but enough to blunt a pure top-end kick and reward someone who can hold power to the line.

Positioning will be fraught: roundabouts at 2,700 m, 1,800 m and 1,200 m, then a very sharp turn with 250 m to go, from which the road kicks up around 2%. That corner is the whole finish — come out of it in front and you are extremely hard to pass.

Unless a strong, committed group gets clear over Paüls, this ends in a sprint.
🚴 Stage 6 — Route Intel
Four categorised climbs and over 3,000 m of climbing, two early and two late — enough vertical to hand the initiative to a strong breakaway rather than the sprinters' teams. The riders leave the coast at Alcossebre and head inland over the Puerto de la Serra.

The talking point is the sterrato: a dirt sector on the final climb, a genuine rarity at the Vuelta. Loose surface, position dependent on nerve as much as legs, and a real chance of a badly-timed mechanical deciding someone's day.
🏁 THE FINAL 21 KM
From the last summit with 20.6 km to go, a technical descent runs south-east back to the flatland — and this time the route bypasses Benicàssim rather than dropping into it. What follows is flat: south-west, south-east, south-west again into Castellón, with the final kilometre drifting fractionally downhill at 0.4%.

If it comes back together, this is a fast, flat, awkward finish. The run-in is a roundabout course — a ring-road roundabout at 4,700 m, then more at 4,200, 3,400 and 1,700 m, then three in quick succession at 1,200, 750 and 200 m. That last one, 200 m out, will scatter a poorly-positioned leadout completely.

A sprint is possible but far from certain. Two late climbs, a dirt sector and a technical run-in give a breakaway more than enough to work with.
🚴 Stage 7 — Route Intel
3,572 m of climbing — the most of any stage so far — packed into under 150 km, with four categorised climbs before the summit finish.

Valdelinares last appeared in 2014, when Winner Anacona soloed clear in lashing rain and Nairo Quintana took the red jersey behind. A short, sharp day where the overall favourites are fully exposed.
🏁 THE FINAL 13.5 KM
The penultimate summit falls at km 136.3, and the 13.5 km that remain open with a short, light descent running east, north and north-east to the foot of the final mountain. Brief — there is no real recovery here.

Aramón Valdelinares (cat. 1, 9.8 km at 5.9%) then heads south-east and north-east, and its average badly understates it. It opens deceptively at 2.6% then 5.2% for the first two kilometres. Then 3.8 km at 7.7% — this is where the selection is made. A false reprieve follows, 2 km at 4–6%, just enough to let a dropped rider believe they are coming back.

They are not, because the sting is last: 2 km at 8.1% then 7.4%, right where the legs are emptiest. And then something unusual — the final 800 m are essentially flat, with only a 2% ramp in the last 200 m.

That flat finish changes everything tactically. An attack on the steep pitches has to be decisive, because a rider who crests still in contact can use those closing metres to come back. Mostly straight, with the few hairpins low down — the last at 4,800 m — then a 90-degree corner at 800 m and a roundabout at 600 m.
